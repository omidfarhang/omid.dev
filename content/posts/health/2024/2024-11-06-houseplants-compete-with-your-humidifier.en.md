Houseplants do release water into the air, and under the right conditions their effect can be measured. In an ordinary home, however, a few plants are unlikely to compete with a humidifier. Their output is slow, variable, and difficult to control; a humidifier is designed to deliver a known amount of water in a short time.

If the goal is a specific indoor humidity level, a hygrometer is a better guide than the number of pots in the room.

## How plants add moisture to a room

Roots take up water and transport it through the plant. Some of that water leaves through small pores in the leaves as vapor, a process called **transpiration**. Water also evaporates directly from the growing medium. Together, these processes are known as evapotranspiration.

There is no dependable output figure for “one houseplant.” The rate changes with:

- species and total leaf area;
- light level and time of day;
- moisture in the growing medium;
- air temperature and relative humidity;
- air movement and ventilation; and
- season and the plant's growth state.

A large, well-watered plant in bright indirect light may release much more water than a small plant of the same species in a dim corner. Claims such as “this plant produces 100 milliliters per day” mean little unless the test conditions are also given.

## What room-scale studies show

A 2024 study in the *Journal of Building Engineering* tested plants in naturally ventilated, unoccupied offices. Adding either 12 *Ficus* plants or six *Epipremnum* plants to a 28-cubic-meter office produced a small but measurable rise in moisture content during spring and winter.

Depending on the season, each plant released about **35 to 68 grams of water per day**. A gram of water is approximately one milliliter. Much of that moisture, however, was removed by air exchange or absorbed by room surfaces. In summer, higher ventilation rates outweighed the plants' greater water output. Air exchange influenced room humidity more than the plants did.

A separate 2024 *PLOS One* study found a much larger change in three offices containing Boston ferns (*Nephrolepis exaltata*). Median relative humidity was 29.1% with no plants, 38.9% with five plants, and 49.2% with 18 plants.

These results are not contradictory. They show how strongly the outcome depends on species, leaf area, plant density, watering, room volume, and ventilation. The Boston fern result demonstrates what can happen in a particular setup; it does not promise that five plants will add ten percentage points of humidity to every room.

## A useful scale comparison

Using the 35–68 milliliters per day measured in the naturally ventilated offices, one plant averaged roughly **1.5–2.8 milliliters per hour**.

A typical small room humidifier may be rated around **200–300 milliliters per hour**. On water output alone, one hour of operation is therefore on the order of **70–200 plant-hours**, depending on which ends of the ranges are compared. Put another way, one plant would need roughly three to eight days to release the amount of water that the appliance can release in one hour.

This is an order-of-magnitude comparison, not a performance guarantee. A humidifier may cycle on and off, and ventilation removes moisture from either source. It nevertheless explains why a few pots cannot provide the speed or control of an appliance.

## Peace lily and rubber plant

A peace lily (*Spathiphyllum*) has broad leaves and generally prefers a lightly moist growing medium. With suitable light, warmth, and water, it can transpire steadily. Its output still cannot be predicted from the species name alone.

A rubber plant (*Ficus elastica*) also loses water through its leaves. Large glossy leaves do not automatically make it an effective humidifier: total leaf area, light, watering, and room conditions remain more important than appearance.

Both are best treated as plants, not climate-control devices. They also require sensible placement. Chewed plant material can irritate the mouth and digestive tract of pets or children, and rubber plant sap may irritate skin. Keep them out of reach where that is a concern.

## Measure before adding moisture

A simple digital hygrometer can establish whether the room is actually dry. Place it away from the humidifier, plants, radiators, direct sunlight, windows, and exterior walls, then look at readings over several hours or days rather than a single moment.

For many homes, **30–50% relative humidity** is a practical target. The appropriate point within that range depends on outdoor temperature, the building, and whether moisture condenses on cold surfaces. More is not always better: persistent humidity above 50% can encourage mold and dust mites.

Condensation on windows, damp patches, or a musty smell are signs to reduce humidification and investigate ventilation, even if a sensor elsewhere reports an acceptable number.

## Which option fits the problem?

| Situation | Practical response |
|---|---|
| Humidity remains between 30% and 50% | No extra humidification is normally needed. |
| It occasionally falls just below 30% | Check heating and ventilation; plants may provide a small background contribution. |
| The room is consistently very dry | Use a properly sized humidifier with a humidistat or separate hygrometer. |
| Many leafy plants occupy a small, low-ventilation room | Monitor humidity; their combined effect may become noticeable. |
| Windows or walls collect condensation | Reduce humidification and address ventilation or cold surfaces. |
| Nobody can maintain the appliance | Do not run a humidifier; neglected standing water is not a good trade-off. |

Plants and a humidifier can coexist, but the appliance should be adjusted according to measured humidity—not an assumed contribution from the plants.

## Humidifier hygiene matters

A humidifier offers much more predictable output, but only when maintained properly. The US Environmental Protection Agency recommends:

- emptying the tank, drying accessible surfaces, and refilling with fresh water daily;
- using low-mineral or distilled water, especially in ultrasonic or impeller units that can disperse minerals as “white dust”;
- cleaning portable units about every third day, following the manufacturer's instructions;
- rinsing away cleaning products completely; and
- reducing or stopping use if relative humidity exceeds 50% or condensation appears.

Place the unit so mist does not settle directly on walls, bedding, wood, or electronics.

## Plants are not air purifiers either

The familiar air-purifying claim comes largely from experiments in small, sealed chambers. Plants can remove some pollutants under those conditions, but ordinary rooms are larger and continually exchange air. Reviews that translate chamber results to real buildings find the effect of a normal number of pots negligible.

For indoor air quality, first reduce pollutant sources and ventilate appropriately. If particles are the problem, a correctly sized HEPA air cleaner may help. Plants still have value as living objects and as part of a pleasant room; they simply should not replace ventilation, filtration, or moisture control.

## The verdict

Houseplants genuinely add water vapor to indoor air. A dense collection of leafy, well-watered plants can produce a measurable change in the right room. But a few peace lilies or rubber plants cannot match a humidifier for speed, capacity, or control.

Choose plants because they suit the light, space, and care you can provide. Treat their contribution to humidity as a variable side effect. When a room needs deliberate humidification, measure it and use a clean, appropriately sized appliance.
